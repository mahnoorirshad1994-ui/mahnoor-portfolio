Case Study 01

Redesigning How Users Search on the Bayut App

CompanyBayut
Year2022
RoleProduct Manager, Consumer Mobile
TypeB2C · Mobile · Experimentation
Scroll to explore
The Context

In property search, relevance is everything. Filters were the primary tool users had to refine results. But accessing them meant leaving the page entirely.

Despite high filter usage overall, accessing filters required users to leave the results page, navigate to a separate screen, apply their criteria, and return. Every extra step was a drop-off risk in a journey where intent was already established. The initial direction was to improve the existing filters screen. I proposed something different: bring the filters to the user rather than sending the user to the filters.

My Hypothesis

If users could apply high-frequency filters without leaving the results page, filter engagement would increase, average leads per user would rise, and time to first lead would fall.

The Approach

A controlled A/B test across Android and iOS — the right method because the change was a significant surface-level intervention. We needed to validate the impact on real users before committing to a full rollout, and isolate the effect cleanly from other variables.

Baseline
Existing experience
Filters accessible only via a separate screen, requiring users to leave the search results page entirely.
Variant
Quick filter bar
High-frequency filters surfaced directly on the search results page. Filters were ordered by usage frequency, placing the most commonly applied criteria first so users could act on them immediately without scrolling.
Tracked
Five metrics
Converted users, average leads per user, filter usage rate, time on search results page, and conversion by filter interaction type.
Design Decision
Applied filter behaviour
We tested two variations once a filter was applied.
Variation A
Applied filter value displayed within the chip itself, keeping the bar compact but making chips appear less interactive.
Variation B
Applied filter moved to the back of the bar, requiring users to scroll left to find and change it. Cleaner visually but added friction to edits.
What Made It Hard
Deciding which filters to surface and in what order
Not all filters were equally useful at the point of browsing. Selecting the wrong ones or ordering them poorly would clutter the interface without adding value. I used usage data to identify the highest-frequency filters and validated both selection and ordering before building.
Interpreting mixed results across platforms
Android and iOS showed different effect sizes. Android showed a stronger response across all metrics, while iOS improvements were smaller but consistent. This required careful framing to stakeholders to avoid either overstating or understating the impact.
The 40% gap
After initial results came in, approximately 40% of users were still not interacting with the filter bar at all. Two likely causes emerged: the chips did not appear visually clickable due to applied values being displayed inside them, and there was insufficient user education about the new interaction. Rather than shipping and moving on, this finding drove a direct second iteration.

Users who used both the quick filter bar and the full filter screen had the highest conversion rate of all segments — 47.3% on Android and 40.4% on iOS. The two were complementary, not competing.

What Changed

Following the second iteration addressing visual affordance and user education, the full rollout delivered:

30%
Improvement in time to first lead
75%
Increase in leads per session
What I Took Away

Most teams would have shipped the first result and called it a win. I chose to ask why 40% of users were ignoring a feature that was clearly working for the other 60%. That question led to an iteration that nearly doubled the lead improvement. The discipline of investigating failure within success — not just reporting the headline number — is what turns a good product decision into a great one.
